**You know an idea is worth building when real customers have shown they'll pay for it, not when friends say it sounds good.** Getting to that point doesn't need a big budget. It needs six steps done in the right order, and the order matters more than most people think.

This is the method we use at TAL, short for The Adda Labs, for our own ideas and for founders who come to us before they build.

## The 6 steps

| Step | What you do | You're done when |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Map the assumptions | Write down every belief the idea depends on | You can point to the 2 or 3 beliefs that would sink it if wrong |
| 2. Talk to real customers | Ask the people who would actually pay | You've heard the problem in their words, not yours |
| 3. Check the market reality | See who else is there and where the gap is | You can say why someone would pick you over the obvious option |
| 4. Test willingness to pay | Find out who will pay, and how much | Someone has named a price, or better, paid a deposit |
| 5. Run the smallest possible test | A pilot or pre-sale that gives a real signal | You have a number, not an opinion |
| 6. Make the call | Go, stop or pivot | The decision is written down, with the evidence next to it |

## The step most people skip

Most people jump straight to step 2. They write a questionnaire, send it to fifty people, and get fifty polite answers that prove nothing.

**Map your assumptions before you write a single question.** If your idea is a cloud kitchen in Kharghar, the beliefs might be: office workers there order lunch online, they'll pay ₹250 a meal, and delivery can stay under 30 minutes. Each belief becomes a question, and each question has a clear "this would prove me wrong" answer. Without that list, you end up asking what's easy to ask instead of what you need to know.

## Talk to people, don't survey them

In step 2, a conversation beats a form. Ask about the last time they had the problem, what they did about it, and what it cost them. Past behaviour tells you far more than "would you use this?", which almost everyone answers with a kind yes.

## Make paying part of the test

Steps 4 and 5 are where ideas usually fall apart, and that's useful. A pre-order, a paid pilot or a refundable deposit tells you more in a week than a month of interviews. If nobody will put money down for the smallest version, the bigger version won't fix that.

## Stopping counts as a result

Step 6 has three outcomes, and "stop" is one of them. An idea that stops after a two-week test has saved months of building and lakhs of spend. We treat that as a win, and we write down why, so the next idea starts smarter.
